# ActivityOS vs iHASCO — benchmark & upgrade report

Deep benchmark of **34 ActivityOS courses** against iHASCO's public course outlines and the governing UK standards (KCSIE 2026, Working Together 2023, FGM Act, Prevent, Modern Slavery Act, HSE, FSA/HACCP, Natasha's Law, RCUK, NICE, UKHSA, NCSC). For each course: what's stronger in ours, what iHASCO covered that we didn't, and the content I added to close the gap. Every gap has been folded into that course as a new "Going further" lesson.

## Safeguarding Children (Level 2)  `c1`
**iHASCO covers:** iHASCO's public "Safeguarding Children – Level 2" course is a ~55–60 minute, CPD/IIRSM-approved module built around five plain sections: Safeguarding Responsibilities, Recognising Abuse, Responding to Abuse, Recording Observations and Reporting Concerns, ending in a 20-question test and covers Care Certificate Standard 11. Its stated learning outcomes are limited to working towards best practice, recognising signs of abuse with a child-centred approach, and the importance of reporting and recording. Notably its public page still cites dated frameworks — Working Together to Safeguard Children 2015 and the Children and Young Persons Act 2008 — rather than the current 2023/2026 guidance.

**Where ours is already better**
- Current and correctly sourced: we are grounded in Working Together to Safeguard Children 2023 and reference KCSIE, plus named modern statutes (Domestic Abuse Act 2021, FGM Act 2003), whereas iHASCO's public outcomes still cite Working Together 2015 and the Children and Young Persons Act 2008.
- Far broader risk coverage: contextual safeguarding, child-on-child abuse, CSE, county lines/CCE, online 'four Cs', Prevent/Channel, FGM, honour-based abuse, forced marriage, modern slavery/NRM and money muling — well beyond iHASCO's generic 'types of abuse'.
- Deeper systems knowledge staff actually use: the thresholds continuum (universal / early help / s17 child in need / s47 significant harm), the DSL/deputy, LADO for allegations against adults, MASH front door, and low-level concerns — iHASCO's outline stops at 'reporting concerns'.
- Genuinely interactive and memory-anchored: a 2-minute motion film, the Notice-Respond-Record-Report spine, the 5 R's with TED prompts, plus sort/match/order/scenario/reveal interactions rather than slides and a single end test.
- Correctly framed for our audience: KCSIE/EYFS content is adopted as good practice for a non-school holiday-camp/activity-club context, with realistic camp and club scenarios rather than classroom examples.

**Where theirs was stronger / we were thin**
- A dedicated, up-front 'Safeguarding Responsibilities/legislation' section that explicitly frames children's rights (UNCRC) and the Equality Act 2010 — our course teaches law inline but never gives learners a clean 'your legal duties and the key statutes' anchor.
- Explicitly teaches 'what happens after you refer' — the post-referral process — which reassures staff; our course explains thresholds but stops at the referral and never shows the strategy discussion, s47 enquiry, child protection conference or plan that follows.
- Clear external credentialing that buyers look for: Care Certificate Standard 11 mapping and IIRSM approval, which we do not surface at all.
- A tidy, confidence-building five-part structure and short run-time that a busy seasonal workforce can finish in one sitting — our course is richer but longer and could feel heavy for a first-day casual.
- Information-sharing and confidentiality is implied in their 'recording/reporting' sections; ours never squarely tackles the Data Protection Act 2018 / UK GDPR 'is it OK to share?' anxiety that stops real staff acting.

**Gaps I closed**
- Information sharing: the 'seven golden rules' from HM Government's Information Sharing advice (updated May 2024) and that the UK GDPR / Data Protection Act 2018 are NOT a barrier — you may share without consent where a child may be at risk. This is the single biggest missing reassurance.
- What happens after a referral: the strategy discussion, s47 enquiry, initial child protection conference (held within 15 working days of the strategy discussion), the child protection plan under one of the four categories, core group and review conferences — so staff know their concern doesn't vanish.
- Food-safety safeguarding for camps that serve food: Natasha's Law (in force 1 Oct 2021), full-ingredient PPDS labelling, the 14 FSA allergens, and having a written allergy/anaphylaxis plan and staff who can use an adrenaline auto-injector — a genuine camp-specific duty absent from our course.
- Safer recruitment basics in plain English: enhanced DBS with a barred-list check for regulated activity, verifying ID and references, the single central record, and the Childcare (Disqualification) Regulations — 'how safe hands get checked' before someone starts.
- KCSIE 2026 online updates we should adopt: the term 'self-generated intimate images' explicitly including AI-generated 'deepfakes', sextortion/financially-motivated coercion, and the NSPCC/IWF 'Report Remove' tool to get under-18 images taken down.
- KCSIE 2026 also names misogyny as a safeguarding risk and strengthens mental health as a safeguarding issue in its own right — worth naming explicitly.
- Whistleblowing named in law: the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 protects staff who raise genuine concerns in good faith, plus the NSPCC whistleblowing advice line (0800 028 0285) — our course mentions 'whistleblowing policy' but never the statute or the external route.
- Key national contacts on one card: 999, non-emergency police 101, the NSPCC helpline for worried adults (0808 800 5000), Childline for children (0800 1111), and the local MASH/front-door — practical detail iHASCO omits and so do we.

**Added lesson:** "Going further — the detail the best providers include" — 10 content blocks.

## Safeguarding (Level 1) Awareness  `c11`
**iHASCO covers:** iHASCO's "Safeguarding Children" (a combined Level 1 & 2, ~55 minutes) is built around the 4 R's — Recognise, Respond, Report, Record — across five sections: Safeguarding Responsibilities, Recognising Abuse in Children, Responding to Abuse, Recording Observations and Reporting Concerns. Its three stated learning outcomes are working towards best practice (citing the Children and Young Persons Act 2008), recognising the signs of abuse with a child-centred approach, and the importance of reporting and recording. It maps to Care Certificate Standard 11, is CPD/IIRSM approved and Skills for Care endorsed, is offered in 40 languages, and ends with a 20-question test and printable certificate.

**Where ours is already better**
- Purpose-built for holiday camps and activity clubs — residentials, mixed ages, changing rooms, group chats and trips — with realistic scenarios set in that world, where iHASCO is generic and care-sector oriented.
- Far deeper on wider and contemporary risks: CSE, child criminal exploitation/county lines, online grooming, sextortion, radicalisation/Prevent, FGM, so-called honour-based abuse, forced marriage and domestic abuse, all with a clear 'victim not offender' framing.
- Active retrieval-practice pedagogy (reveal cards, sort, match, ordered steps and branching scenarios) across six lessons, not just passive reading plus multiple-choice.
- Genuinely practical on disclosures (Receive/Reassure/React/Record/Refer, TED open prompts, never promising secrecy) and on the human barriers, bystander effect and staff self-care that cause real-world failures.
- Every claim is tied to a correctly named current standard (Working Together 2023, Children Act 1989 paramountcy, Domestic Abuse Act 2021) with accurate helplines, and it is crisp about the Level-1 role: recognise and report, never investigate.

**Where theirs was stronger / we were thin**
- iHASCO devotes a whole 'Safeguarding Responsibilities' section to the practitioner's place in the multi-agency system; ours under-explains that wider machinery (the three safeguarding partners, MASH front door, the section 11 duty).
- It maps to a recognised competency and accreditation framework (Care Certificate Standard 11, CPD/IIRSM, Skills for Care endorsement), giving learners portable, auditable credit — ours carries no accreditation or standard-mapping statement.
- It handles concerns and allegations about staff within 'responsibilities'; our course only name-drops the LADO and never teaches the harm-test threshold or the staff-allegation route.
- Accessibility: iHASCO ships in 40 languages for EAL, low-literacy and diverse workforces, whereas ours is English-only and prose-heavy.
- It runs on one clean, memorable spine (the 4 R's) applied consistently, while ours — though richer — is denser and could lose a first-time learner.

**Gaps I closed**
- The section 11 Children Act 2004 duty to safeguard, and the Working Together 2023 multi-agency system: the three safeguarding partners (local authority, integrated care board, police) that replaced Local Safeguarding Children Boards, with concerns reaching social care via a MASH 'front door'.
- Information-sharing law: that the UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018 do NOT prevent sharing to protect a child, plus the seven golden rules and sharing without consent where necessary to keep a child safe.
- Managing allegations/concerns about staff: the LADO route, the four-limb 'harm test' threshold, and the one-working-day referral expectation — never investigating a colleague yourself.
- Position-of-trust law for coaches/instructors (Sexual Offences Act 2003 as amended by the Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act 2022) — a sexual relationship with a 16 or 17-year-old you coach is a criminal offence regardless of consent; highly relevant to activity clubs.
- The FGM mandatory reporting duty under section 5B of the FGM Act 2003 (a personal duty on regulated teachers and health/social-care professionals to report known FGM in under-18s to the police), plus the NSPCC FGM helpline 0800 028 3550.
- Safer recruitment and DBS: that supervising children is usually 'regulated activity' needing an enhanced DBS plus children's-barred-list check under the Safeguarding Vulnerable Groups Act 2006, and that it is an offence to employ a barred person.
- Safe working practices / code of conduct: one-to-one situations, appropriate physical contact, intimate care, transporting children, and photography/social-media boundaries.
- Early help and contextual safeguarding as concepts (harm beyond the home, where camps and trips sit), plus statutory whistleblowing protection under the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998.

**Added lesson:** "Going further — the detail the best providers include" — 10 content blocks.

## Safeguarding (Level 3) — DSL  `c12`
**iHASCO covers:** iHASCO's "Safeguarding Children — Level 3" is built around a clean five-part spine — Responsibilities, Recognising, Responding, Recording, Reporting — aimed at DSLs/DSOs. Its stated learning outcomes are narrow and practical: be able to recognise abuse, know how to make a record and manage a child-protection file, and understand the DSL role and responsibilities. It leans on teaching the types of abuse and neglect, radicalisation and warning signs, parent/carer health, and online safety, and concludes with a single-sitting 20-question multiple-choice test and printable certificate.

**Where ours is already better**
- Far deeper on the statutory machinery than iHASCO's five-section outline — s17 vs s47, the 15-working-day ICPC, the 45-working-day single assessment, strategy discussion, core group, review-conference cadence, EPO/police protection and the Public Law Outline — so our DSL can actually contribute at conference, not just refer.
- A complete Part 4 leadership competency iHASCO barely touches: the four-limb harm threshold, the one-working-day LADO referral, 'suspension is never automatic', the separate low-level-concerns log, and the legal DBS referral duty (including when someone resigns first).
- Written for the real audience — holiday camps and activity clubs — with off-site trips, alternative provision, contractors/visitors, agency staff and ratios framed as our own practice, rather than a school-shaped generic course.
- Genuinely rigorous pedagogy and assessment: scenario decisions, sort/match/order/reveal interactions, an 85% pass mark and a 25-question final exam versus iHASCO's 20-question MCQ.
- Specialist harms handled with the correct statutory tools named — contextual safeguarding, county lines with NRM and the s45 Modern Slavery defence, FGM/FMPO, MARAC and Operation Encompass, financially-motivated sextortion and fabricated/induced illness — well beyond a warning-signs overview.

**Where theirs was stronger / we were thin**
- A systematic 'Recognising' block that teaches the four categories of abuse (physical, emotional, sexual, neglect) and their warning signs head-on. Ours assumes this from Levels 1–2 and never consolidates the indicators, which a standalone Level-3 buyer will expect to see.
- A tidy, memorable spine — the five Rs — that is easy to retain and audit against. Ours is 20 lessons and roughly two hours; thorough, but it can overwhelm and lacks a single organising mnemonic.
- 'Parent/carer health' as an explicit, named toolkit item (parental mental ill-health, substance misuse and domestic abuse — the multiple-adversity or 'toxic trio' picture). Ours scatters these across lessons rather than framing the parental-risk cluster as one thing to assess.
- A digestible, single-sitting CPD format with an instant printable certificate — friendlier for busy seasonal staff than our deliberately demanding multi-sitting design.
- Tight version currency mapped to the live KCSIE edition. Our course still cites KCSIE 2025, which is superseded by KCSIE 2026 on 1 September 2026, and refers to the mandatory-reporting law as a 'Bill'.

**Gaps I closed**
- Update every reference from KCSIE 2025 to KCSIE 2026 (in force 1 September 2026) and fold in its changes: all staff are now expected to read Part One in full; strengthened duties on mental health and suicidal ideation; misogyny/violence against women and girls named as a driver of harmful sexual behaviour; refreshed filtering-and-monitoring and emerging-technology (incl. AI) expectations; and closer alignment with the EYFS and Working Together.
- Correct and modernise the mandatory-reporting content: it is now the Crime and Policing Act 2026 (Royal Assent 29 April 2026), not a Bill. The duty falls on people in regulated activity to report child sexual abuse that is disclosed or witnessed; failing to report is NOT itself a criminal offence (it is dealt with via the DBS/professional regulator), but preventing or deterring a report IS a new criminal offence, and commencement is expected around 2027.
- Add the Working Together 2023 reform language: 'Family Help' (merging targeted early help and child-in-need support into one offer), the 'lead practitioner', and the national multi-agency child protection standards — ours still speaks only of 'early help' and 'team around the family'.
- Consolidate a recognition refresher of the four categories of abuse (physical, emotional, sexual, neglect) with current indicators, noting that neglect is the most common category for children on a child-protection plan — the exact content iHASCO teaches and ours omits.
- Add abuse linked to faith or belief (e.g. so-called spirit possession or 'witchcraft' branding) — a KCSIE Annex B specific issue that is entirely absent from our course.
- Add so-called 'honour'-based abuse (HBA) as an umbrella that includes breast ironing/flattening, sitting alongside the FGM and forced-marriage content we already have; name breast ironing explicitly as a form of physical abuse.
- Name the Voyeurism (Offences) Act 2019 for upskirting (currently referenced only generically), and spell out the personal FGM reporting duty under s5B of the FGM Act 2003 — a legal duty on the individual regulated professional to report known under-18 cases to the police, not just on the organisation.
- State the DSL currency requirement: KCSIE expects formal DSL training to be refreshed at least every two years with knowledge and skills topped up (via briefings/bulletins) at least annually; and put the NSPCC helpline (0808 800 5000) and whistleblowing advice line (0800 028 0285) in policy so any adult can raise a concern outside the setting.

**Added lesson:** "Staying current — the 2026 updates and the detail the best providers include" — 9 content blocks.

## County Lines & CCE  `c4`
**iHASCO covers:** iHASCO's "Child Exploitation Awareness" is a single 45-minute, CPD-certified course (three sections, 20-question test, 40+ languages) that covers BOTH child sexual exploitation (CSE) and child criminal exploitation (CCE) together. It teaches definitions, grooming tactics, at-risk groups, the concept of consent, warning signs and the long-term impact on victims, then a "Taking action" section on responding to disclosures, recording, reporting and providing ongoing support. It is deliberately broad and generic rather than UK-procedure-specific.

**Where ours is already better**
- Far deeper UK legal and procedural grounding than iHASCO's generic treatment: Modern Slavery Act 2015 s45 statutory defence WITH its Schedule 4 limits, the National Referral Mechanism and duty-to-notify/MS1 distinction, and the KCSIE victim-first principle are all taught explicitly.
- Rich, operational county-lines detail iHASCO does not go near: deal line, cuckooing, plugging, trapping, debt bondage and 'taxing', 'clean skins', 'going country' and contextual safeguarding — the actual mechanics staff will encounter.
- Highly practical, setting-specific 'what NOT to do' guidance (never search a child or their bag, never confiscate drugs/cash/weapons, 999 for a weapon) that is exactly right for holiday-club and activity staff and is missing from iHASCO's outline.
- Genuinely interactive pedagogy — match, order, sort, reveal cards, multi-step scenarios and a capstone scenario — versus iHASCO's linear content plus a 20-question test, so knowledge is applied not just watched.
- A dedicated 'victim-first lens' lesson with myth-busting and scenarios, embedding that a child can never consent to their own exploitation as a whole-course spine rather than a single line.

**Where theirs was stronger / we were thin**
- iHASCO teaches CSE as well as CCE in one course, including CSE-specific signs and the sexual-violence dimension; ours is CCE-only and treats CSE mainly as an overlap footnote for girls, so a learner leaves ours less equipped to spot sexual exploitation.
- iHASCO has an explicit block on the long-term consequences and impact on victims (health, development, trauma); ours is strong on response but barely teaches the lasting harm or trauma bonding, which only surfaces in a quiz-answer.
- iHASCO devotes part of its 'Taking action' section to providing ongoing support and helping a child feel safe, valued and empowered over time; ours largely stops at the referral, underplaying the front-line worker's continuing 'trusted adult' role.
- iHASCO consolidates at-risk populations and risk factors into a clear teaching point; ours scatters vulnerabilities through the grooming steps and never gives staff a single 'who is at higher risk' reference.
- iHASCO's single-course, 40+ language, CPD-certified format is an easier universal-induction product; ours assumes prior Level 2 safeguarding, so a brand-new volunteer could hit gaps we assume are already covered.

**Gaps I closed**
- Update the flagship legal content: our 'law' callout still calls it the Crime and Policing BILL 2025 'progressing through Parliament' with 'proposed' penalties. It is now the Crime and Policing Act 2026 (Royal Assent 29 April 2026) with three commenced offences — CCE (max 10 years), cuckooing/home-takeover (max 5 years) and internal concealment ('plugging'), plus CCE prevention orders and automatic special measures for child victims. This is our single most important accuracy fix.
- Refresh the scale statistic: replace/augment the 2019 '27,000 at high risk' stat with the Home Office end-of-year 2025 NRM data — criminal exploitation is now the most common reason children are referred (about 50%, ~3,485), with a record 23,411 total referrals and most child referrals being UK nationals.
- Add a consolidated 'who is at higher risk' reference (SEND/EHCP, care experience, exclusion/absence, poverty, ACEs, prior missing episodes, isolation), framed as good practice we adopt from KCSIE 2025 — currently these vulnerabilities are scattered and never listed together.
- Teach the CCE/CSE overlap properly with actual CSE warning signs, not just a footnote about girls, so staff can recognise sexual exploitation when it co-occurs.
- Add money-muling / child financial exploitation detail (handing over bank cards/'squares', 'easy money' ads, fraud and crypto transfers) — a fast-growing, easily-missed form our course only names in passing.
- Teach long-term impact and trauma bonding as explicit content, explaining why exploited children defend their exploiters, so staff don't misread loyalty as evidence the child is safe.
- Add the front-line worker's ongoing 'trusted adult' role after referral — continuity, non-judgement and keeping the relationship open — which our course underplays by largely ending at the referral.
- Add setting-level procedures our course omits: the local MACE/exploitation panel pathway, recording on the safeguarding system, statutory return-home interviews, the LADO/allegations-against-staff route, and displaying key helplines (NSPCC 0808 800 5000, Fearless/Crimestoppers 0800 555 111, 999/101, local MASH).

**Added lesson:** "Going further — the current law and the detail the best providers include" — 10 content blocks.

## Child Sexual Exploitation (CSE)  `c16`
**iHASCO covers:** iHASCO's "Child Exploitation Awareness" is a single 45-minute module covering both Child Sexual Exploitation (CSE) and Child Criminal Exploitation (CCE) across three sections (Understanding child exploitation, Types of exploitation, Taking action). Its stated outcomes are to identify children at risk, understand what CSE and CCE are, and learn the warning signs, consequences and how to report. It cites the Children Act 1989, Safeguarding Vulnerable Groups Act 2006, Children and Young Persons Act and Child Abduction Act, and ends with a 20-question test and certificate.

**Where ours is already better**
- Far deeper on grooming mechanics: a full stages-of-grooming model, the 'boyfriend/loverboy' tactic, and dedicated interactive sort/reveal exercises that teach staff to tell exploitation from a genuine healthy teenage relationship - iHASCO only lists warning signs.
- A current, standalone online-CSE and sextortion lesson built around the live NCA financially-motivated-sextortion alert, with precise image-handling rules (never view/copy/share/delete), and the CEOP, Internet Watch Foundation and Childline Report Remove routes named - detail iHASCO's outline does not show.
- Legally precise rather than a generic statute list: names the Sexual Offences Act 2003 (under-13 rape, ss.47-50 exploitation offences, positions of trust extended to sports coaches and faith leaders in 2022) and KCSIE 2025's 'a child can never consent to their own exploitation'.
- Strong victim-focused content iHASCO's outline lacks: a whole lesson on myths and victim-blaming language, higher-risk groups (SEND, looked-after children, missing children), why boys are missed, and return-home interviews.
- Much heavier assessment and practice: a 22-question final bank plus scenario, order, match and sort blocks embedded in each lesson, versus a single 20-question test.

**Where theirs was stronger / we were thin**
- Covers Child Criminal Exploitation (CCE) - county lines, drug running, being made to commit crime - as a co-equal exploitation type; our course is CSE-only and mentions CCE just in passing, even though in practice the two overlap constantly.
- Frames 'child exploitation' as one broad phenomenon with a shared grooming model, which is a more realistic single-onboarding picture for staff than a CSE-in-isolation module.
- Anchors exploitation in the wider legal framework (Children Act 1989, Safeguarding Vulnerable Groups Act 2006, Child Abduction Act) - useful context our course does not set out.
- A single 45-minute all-exploitation course is a more efficient mandatory-training footprint than a CSE-only module for small activity providers.
- Its 'Taking action' section is pitched as general reporting for any exploitation type, whereas ours is written tightly around sexual-image and disclosure scenarios.

**Gaps I closed**
- The new mandatory reporting duty for child sexual abuse under the Crime and Policing Act 2026 (Royal Assent 29 April 2026): a statutory duty on people in roles working with children to report CSA when a child or perpetrator discloses it or they witness it, plus a new offence of preventing/deterring a report - our course predates this and does not mention it at all.
- Child Criminal Exploitation, county lines and cuckooing as now-standalone offences under the Crime and Policing Act 2026 (CCE, adult 18+, up to 10 years; cuckooing/home takeover up to 5 years), and how CSE and CCE frequently co-occur - our course only alludes to 'criminal exploitation' once.
- The National Referral Mechanism explained properly: what it is, that trafficked/exploited children are potential victims of modern slavery under the Modern Slavery Act 2015, and that only 'first responder' organisations make NRM referrals (usually via the DSL/local authority) - we name the NRM once without explaining it.
- The authoritative DfE 2017 CSE definition built on an 'imbalance of power', and Working Together to Safeguard Children 2023 as the statutory multi-agency framework (three safeguarding partners, MASH) that sits above KCSIE - our course leans on KCSIE alone.
- Contextual safeguarding (Firmin) - assessing and responding to risk beyond the family/home - and the lawful basis to share information without consent to protect a child (Working Together 2023, s.11 Children Act 2004, Data Protection Act 2018/UK GDPR); staff wrongly believe data-protection law stops them sharing.
- Where a setting's intelligence goes: local MACE (Multi-Agency Child Exploitation) meetings and the Home Office Child Exploitation Disruption Toolkit, and why recording vehicles, locations and adults matters even without a disclosure.
- The LADO route: when the concern is about an adult working with children (a colleague, coach or volunteer), the referral goes to the Local Authority Designated Officer, not the usual children's-services channel - our disclosure lesson only covers concerns about the child's outside abuser.
- Correct statutory terminology: the Serious Crime Act 2015 removed the terms 'child prostitution' and 'child pornography' from law - there is no such thing as a child 'prostitute'; these are sexually exploited children and indecent images of children.

**Added lesson:** "Going further - the duties, wider law and multi-agency system the best providers teach" — 9 content blocks.

## FGM Awareness & Mandatory Reporting  `c17`
**iHASCO covers:** iHASCO's "FGM Awareness & Prevention" is a short (~20 minute), CPD-accredited course built in two sections: Section 1 "FGM Awareness" (what FGM is, cultural contexts, medical considerations, legal status and effects on the victim) and Section 2 "FGM Prevention" (spotting warning signs, safeguarding duties and how to respond). Its three headline learning outcomes are recognising the signs of FGM as part of a safeguarding duty, understanding what FGM is and how it affects victims, and understanding the "4 Rs" of child protection. It ends with a 20-question multiple-choice assessment and printable certificate and is offered in 40 languages, aimed largely at education and regulated staff.

**Where ours is already better**
- Ours is correctly tailored to non-mandated activity/holiday-club staff: it makes the crucial distinction that general activity workers are NOT bound by the section 5B duty and discharge their responsibility by reporting to the DSL without delay, rather than personally calling the police - a nuance iHASCO's education-focused '4 Rs' framing glosses over.
- Ours states the law with real precision and names the standards: FGM Act 2003 (up to 14 years), the section 3A failure-to-protect offence limited to a person 'responsible for' a girl under 16 (up to 7 years), FGM Protection Orders under the Serious Crime Act 2015 (breach up to 5 years) and the section 5B mandatory reporting duty - iHASCO only summarises 'legal status'.
- Ours drills the exact edge cases where the section 5B duty is and is not triggered (current-age-under-18 test, direct disclosure vs observation, third-party disclosure not triggering it, over-18 disclosures, mere suspicion) using interactive match/sort blocks - far more granular decision-making than a 20-minute overview.
- Ours contains multiple realistic activity-setting scenarios (summer holiday club, imminent 'cutting season' travel, historic FGM disclosed by a 16-year-old) with graded right/wrong feedback, plus 999-imminent-risk escalation - practical rehearsal iHASCO's brief questionnaire format does not match.
- Ours grounds every figure and myth-busts accurately: cited prevalence (137,000 in England and Wales; 230 million worldwide, UNICEF 2024), the four WHO types in a table, and a clear statement that FGM predates and cuts across faiths and is not a religious requirement.

**Where theirs was stronger / we were thin**
- iHASCO badges a single memorable spine for the response - the '4 Rs' of child protection (Recognise, Respond, Report, Record) - which our course teaches in substance but never names as a mnemonic staff can recall under pressure.
- iHASCO is delivered in 40 languages with a strong accessibility/engagement focus, giving genuinely better reach across a diverse, multilingual workforce than our single-language course.
- iHASCO packages a formal 20-question assessment with a printable CPD certificate as a clear, marketable completion artefact - a positioning strength for buyers even though our quiz is more rigorous.
- iHASCO keeps the whole thing to roughly 20 minutes, a deliberate brevity/completion advantage for time-poor seasonal staff that our six-lesson course does not lead on.
- iHASCO-style courses typically signpost a concrete external helpline for advice; ours currently gives no single anonymous resource a worried worker can ring.

**Gaps I closed**
- The NSPCC FGM Helpline - 0800 028 3550 (free, 24/7, you can stay anonymous) and fgmhelp@nspcc.org.uk - as a concrete resource for advice when a worker is unsure, which our course currently omits.
- The 'one chance rule': a potential victim may have only one opportunity to ask for help and you may have only one chance to give it, so getting the first response right is critical - a principle our course implies but never states.
- An explicit, named '4 Rs' framework (Recognise, Respond, Report, Record) so our response has the same memorable structure the best courses use.
- The Multi-agency statutory guidance on FGM (2016, updated 2018/2019) as the named national standard that directs how agencies, the police and children's social care work together on FGM.
- Related harmful practices staff should recognise alongside FGM and handle the same way - breast flattening/ironing, honour-based abuse and forced marriage - which sit in the same safeguarding family but are absent from our course.
- The duty to safeguard OTHER children in the household: an adult or third-party disclosure that does not trigger section 5B still means younger sisters may be at risk, so we still refer - a point that closes the 'not triggered = do nothing' trap.
- What actually happens after a report - a joint police and children's social care strategy discussion, a possible FGM Protection Order, and specialist medical assessment - so staff trust that escalating protects the child rather than causing harm.
- That failure by a mandated colleague to comply with section 5B is not itself a criminal offence but is dealt with through fitness-to-practise / disciplinary referral to their regulator, and that our setting should name FGM in its child protection policy with a DSL and deputy, adopting KCSIE 2026 and Working Together to Safeguard Children 2023 as good practice (we are not a school).

**Added lesson:** "Going further - the detail the best providers include" — 9 content blocks.

## The Prevent Duty  `c6`
**iHASCO covers:** iHASCO's "Prevent Duty Training" is a short (~20-minute) course aimed at education and childcare staff. Its stated learning outcomes are to understand what the Prevent duty is and the definitions of terrorism and extremism, to learn the "4 Rs" of child protection, and to know how to respond to, report and record cases of radicalisation. Structurally it is built around the Notice–Check–Share procedure: an opening section on what makes someone susceptible, the differences between younger and older children and the behaviours to look out for, then a closing section on checking and sharing concerns, ending with a 10-question test and printable certificate.

**Where ours is already better**
- Ours is anchored in the exact law and current statutory guidance — s26 Counter-Terrorism and Security Act 2015, the 2023 Prevent duty guidance in force 31 Dec 2023, and the Shawcross review — rather than generic 'the Prevent duty says'.
- Ours teaches the full CONTEST four-Ps model, the three strategic objectives, and explains the 'vulnerable'→'susceptible' language change and why it matters, which iHASCO's outcomes do not mention.

**Gaps I closed**

- Safe defrosting/thawing: thaw frozen raw meat and poultry fully in the fridge (not at room temperature), make sure it is completely defrosted before cooking (ice in the centre prevents proper cooking), keep thawing juices off other food, and never refreeze raw food once thawed.
- Bacterial spores and toxins, including reheated rice: some bacteria (e.g. Bacillus cereus in cooked rice, Clostridium perfringens) survive cooking as spores and produce toxins if food is left warm — cool cooked rice quickly (within 1 hour), chill it, and reheat only once until steaming hot.
- Enforcement powers under the Food Safety and Hygiene (England) Regulations 2013 and Food Safety Act 1990: an EHO can serve a Hygiene Improvement Notice, a Hygiene Emergency Prohibition Notice that closes premises/stops a process immediately where there is a risk to health, seize food, and prosecute — with unlimited fines and, in the worst cases, imprisonment.
- Waste and pest control detail: keep waste in lidded, foot-operated bins, remove it regularly, keep external areas tidy, and recognise signs of pests (droppings, gnaw marks, nests, dead insects) — report and act, don't ignore, because pests spread disease and are an enforcement trigger.
- Gloves and PPE realism: single-use gloves are not a substitute for handwashing and must be changed between raw and ready-to-eat tasks (and when torn); avoid strong perfume, and remove watches and nail varnish/false nails, which can flake or harbour bacteria.

- 'Stop, drop and roll' — what to do if someone's clothing catches fire — is not taught
- No benchmark for how often to run and record fire drills, and no mention of taking the register/signing-in book to the assembly point so visitors and contractors are also accounted for

## Off-site Visits & Trip Safety  `c25`
**iHASCO covers:** iHASCO's "School Trips for Organisers & Support" is a 45-minute, IIRSM-approved course built around four linear modules: Planning a School Trip, Health & Safety on a School Trip, The Trip, and Residential Trips & Trips Abroad, ending in a 10-question test. It is pitched at schools and Educational Visits Coordinators and leans on brief in-course questionnaires and a compliance framing ("show commitment to relevant legislation"). Its notable strengths are a dedicated residential/abroad module, explicit transport-type comparison, and structured behaviour/communication and incident-reporting content — but it is school-centric and light on the outdoor-sector detail (AALS, OEAP categories, provider vetting).

**Where ours is already better**
- Written specifically for holiday-camp and activity-club staff in plain English, not school teachers — so ratios, buddy system and headcounts land for a non-school setting where iHASCO assumes an EVC and school hierarchy.
- Far richer interaction than iHASCO's brief questionnaires: reveal cards, ordered sequences, sort-into-buckets, matching, staged 'steps' and branching scenarios drive genuine engagement and retention.
- Strong, correctly-framed risk-benefit approach grounded in OEAP National Guidance — teaches staff to manage rather than eliminate risk, which iHASCO only implies.
- A detailed, rehearsable missing-child procedure (alert and account, hold the group with a named adult, tell the venue, escalate to 999, record) that is more operationally concrete than iHASCO's coverage.
- Practical supervision discipline others skip: count by name at boarding, headcounts at every transition, kerbside drill for boarding/alighting, and medication physically travelling with the group.

**Where theirs was stronger / we were thin**
- A full standalone module on residential trips and trips abroad — passports, documentation, parental communication and enhanced safeguarding — where ours compresses all of this into a single info callout.
- Explicit legal-compliance framing as a learning outcome ('understand and show commitment to relevant legislation'); ours names DfE and OEAP but never states who is legally responsible or under which Act.
- A clear comparison of transport types (coach, minibus, private vehicle) with the practical trade-offs of each — ours covers transport safety but not the choice of vehicle or its legal implications.
- Structured behaviour/code-of-conduct expectations plus explicit communication protocols and rendezvous points as taught content, reinforcing pupil-management, not just headcounting.
- A defined emergency and incident-reporting flow within its Health & Safety module, giving learners a 'what to report and to whom' structure after an incident.

**Gaps I closed**
- The course never names who is legally responsible: under the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 the duty sits with the employer (the organisation), which must ensure children's safety so far as is reasonably practicable; the visit leader and EVC act on its behalf. Currently ours only cites DfE/OEAP guidance, not the statutory duty.
- No mention of the non-delegable duty of care / vicarious liability — an organisation cannot offload its duty of care by hiring a third-party provider (the principle confirmed by the Supreme Court in Woodland v Essex CC, 2013). - Trips abroad specifics are missing entirely: valid passports and travel documents, GHIC and adequate travel insurance, the pan-European emergency number 112, checking FCDO travel advice, and a base contact reachable across time zones.
- Transport legalities are absent: a minibus needs a driver with D1 entitlement or a Section 19/22 permit for non-profit use, seatbelts must be worn with age-appropriate restraints, and PSVs/minibuses are legally required to carry a first-aid kit.
- First-aid provision is never quantified — OEAP guidance requires the visit leader to assess, as part of the risk assessment, the level of first-aid cover and kit appropriate to the group, activity and remoteness; ours mentions carrying a kit but not sizing the provision or having a qualified first-aider.
- OEAP's categories of visit and EVC competence are not taught: higher-risk 'Category C' visits (adventurous, residential, abroad, or planned outdoor use of water) need extra scrutiny/approval, and OEAP recommends the EVC hold accredited EVC training — ours mentions the EVC role but not the graded approval that flows from it. After-trip actions (debrief, recording, and reporting reportable incidents under RIDDOR / informing your local authority or insurer) are also missing.

**Added lesson:** "Legal duties, providers and trips abroad" — 10 content blocks.

## Equality, Diversity & Inclusion  `c31`
**iHASCO covers:** iHASCO's "Equality, Diversity, and Inclusion (EDI)" is a generic ~60-minute workplace course in four sections (what EDI means; why EDI/barriers; the protected characteristics; EDI in practice), closing with a 20-question test and a printable CPD certificate, and available in 40 languages. - Practical, safeguarding-linked procedure for what to do when a discriminatory incident happens - stop it, record it factually in the child's words, report to the lead, follow up - which iHASCO's page does not surface.

- Explicitly teaches allyship / being an active bystander - noticing, speaking up and backing colleagues and under-represented people - which ours only implies.
- Makes the positive organisational and business case for diversity (benefits of a diverse, supported team), giving learners a 'why it pays' as well as a 'why it's law'.

- Does not state that the setting is also responsible for acting on discrimination or harassment by third parties on site - other children, other parents, volunteers and contractors - not just its own staff.
- Confidentiality is missing: a child's disability or gender-reassignment history must not be shared without consent, and gender reassignment is protected with no medical process or minimum age required.
- The reasonable-adjustments duty is not framed as three specific statutory requirements (change a provision/criterion/practice; overcome a physical feature; provide an auxiliary aid or service), and the 'knowledge' point (you must know or reasonably be expected to know a child is disabled) is not covered.

- Explicitly teaches the effects and impact of bullying, which motivates urgency; ours describes what to do but never spells out the harm bullying causes.

## Working with Parents: Communication & Complaints  `c39`

**Where ours is already better**

**Where theirs was stronger / we were thin**
