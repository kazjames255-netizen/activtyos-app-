## 0. About this template

This is a **template**, not legal advice. It has been drafted to be a realistic starting point for a UK out-of-school activity provider, but no template can know your registration status, your local safeguarding partnership's procedures, the ages you work with, or the contracts you have signed. Before you adopt it, you must read it in full, complete every `[PLACEHOLDER]`, resolve every `[CHECK: …]` note, and have it reviewed and signed off by someone competent — normally your Designated Safeguarding Lead, and where the stakes are high, a solicitor or your local authority's safeguarding-in-education / out-of-school settings team. Adopting an unedited template is worse than having no policy at all, because it tells an inspector, a parent or a court that you did not think about your own setting.

## 1. Purpose

1.1 Every child who takes part in our activities has the right to be safe, and to be treated with dignity and respect. Safeguarding is not a form that gets filed; it is the ordinary business of noticing, recording and acting.

1.2 This policy sets out:

- what we do to prevent harm to children in our care;
- how everyone working for us recognises and responds to a concern;
- who is accountable, by name;
- the exact steps, routes and timescales for reporting a concern — internally and to external agencies;
- how we handle an allegation or a concern about one of our own adults;
- what we record, where it lives, who can see it and how long we keep it.

1.3 It is written so that a coach on their first shift can find the answer to "something has just happened — what do I do?" in under a minute. Sections 11 and 12 are the operational core; everything else supports them.

1.4 Our overriding principle: **the child's welfare comes first.** Where this policy conflicts with any commercial, reputational or contractual interest of this organisation, this policy wins.

## 3. The legal and guidance framework

3.1 This section says which instruments apply to us and — just as importantly — which do not. Getting this right matters: an out-of-school activity provider is not a school, and claiming statutory duties you do not have is as unhelpful as ignoring ones you do.

### 3.2 What applies to everyone working with children

**Children Act 1989.** The foundation of child protection law in England and Wales. Two thresholds run through this policy:

- **section 17** — a "child in need", where a child is unlikely to achieve or maintain a reasonable standard of health or development without services from the local authority;
- **section 47** — where the local authority has reasonable cause to suspect a child is suffering, or is likely to suffer, **significant harm**, it must make enquiries.

Both duties sit on the **local authority**, not on us. Our job is to notice, record, and refer so that the local authority can decide. We do not investigate, and we do not have to be sure before we refer. (See §12.4.)

**Children Act 2004.** Introduced the duty on certain agencies to make arrangements to safeguard and promote the welfare of children, and underpins the multi-agency safeguarding arrangements in each local area.

> `[CHECK: whether your setting falls within the "relevant agency" arrangements made by your local safeguarding partners. Out-of-school activity providers are generally not among the agencies with a statutory section 11 duty, but local safeguarding partnerships can designate relevant agencies locally, and a commissioned provider may be brought in contractually. Confirm with your local partnership rather than assuming either way.]`

**Working Together to Safeguard Children.** The statutory multi-agency guidance for England. It is explicit that safeguarding is everybody's responsibility and that its expectations reach organisations and agencies working with children, not only those with a named statutory duty. It sets out the referral routes, the multi-agency thresholds and the principles of information sharing that this policy follows.

> `[CHECK: which edition is current. The 2023 edition was a substantial rewrite and is the one this template is built around, but this guidance is updated periodically. Cite the edition in force on the date you adopt this policy, and re-check at each annual review.]`

**Safeguarding Vulnerable Groups Act 2006 and the Disclosure and Barring Service.** Defines **regulated activity** with children, creates the children's barred list, makes it an offence to knowingly engage a barred person in regulated activity, and places a **duty to refer** to the DBS on an employer who removes a person from regulated activity because they have harmed, or may harm, a child. See §6 and §13.11.

**Sexual Offences Act 2003 — abuse of a position of trust.** It is a criminal offence for an adult in a defined position of trust to engage in sexual activity with a 16- or 17-year-old in their care, even though that young person is over the age of consent. The categories of position of trust were **extended to include those who coach, teach, train or instruct in a sport or a religion**, which brings a great many activity providers squarely within scope.

> `[CHECK: the exact amending provision and commencement date. The extension was made by the Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act 2022, inserting a new position-of-trust category into the Sexual Offences Act 2003, and took effect during 2022. Confirm the section number and date before citing it in a disciplinary or training context — the substance is not in doubt, the citation should be verified.]`

**Data Protection Act 2018 and UK GDPR.** Govern how we hold and share safeguarding information. Data protection law is **not** a barrier to sharing information to safeguard a child. See §23.

**Equality Act 2010.** We make reasonable adjustments so that disabled children can take part. Disabled children, children with SEND, and children with communication differences are at **greater** risk of abuse and less likely to be believed. §10.9 addresses this specifically.

### 3.3 The guidance that actually bites for camps and clubs

**DfE, "Keeping children safe in out-of-school settings" (the out-of-school settings code of practice).** This is the guidance written *for us*. It is non-statutory, but it is the document an inspector, a local authority, a school partner or an insurer will measure us against, and it is the practical backbone of this policy. It expects an out-of-school setting to have, as a minimum:

- a written safeguarding and child protection policy that staff and parents can actually get hold of;
- a named **Designated Safeguarding Lead** whom every member of staff can identify and reach;
- safer recruitment, including appropriate DBS checks;
- staff trained to recognise and report a concern;
- clear reporting routes to children's social care and the police;
- suitable staffing ratios, supervision, health and safety, and premises arrangements;
- information for parents about how we keep children safe.

> `[CHECK: the current title and publication date of this guidance, and whether the accompanying "voluntary safeguarding code of practice" material has been updated or consolidated. DfE has revised this guidance since first publication. Also check whether an equivalent exists for your nation if you are not in England — at the time of drafting, this guidance is England-only.]`

### 3.4 Where we work with schools

3.4.1 **Keeping Children Safe in Education (KCSIE)** is statutory guidance for **schools and colleges**. It is not statutory for an out-of-school activity provider in its own right.

3.4.2 It nevertheless matters to us in three ways:

- **On school sites and under school contracts.** A school's own KCSIE duties do not stop when it hands its hall to us or commissions us to run wraparound or extended provision. Schools discharge those duties partly by imposing requirements on providers, contractually. Where we work with a school we will comply with the school's safeguarding arrangements as well as this policy, agree in writing who holds the DSL role for our sessions, and know how to contact the school's DSL. See §24.
- **As the recognised standard of practice.** We adopt KCSIE's approach voluntarily because it is the clearest articulation of good practice in England. In particular we follow **Part 1** (what all staff need to know) as our induction standard, and **Part 4** (concerns and allegations about adults who work with children) as our procedure in §13.
- **Because our staff move between sectors.** Many of our coaches also work in schools and will already know the KCSIE framework.

3.4.3 We do not claim to be bound by KCSIE where we are not, and we do not tell parents or partners that we are "KCSIE compliant" as though it were a certificate. We say, accurately, that we follow it.

> `[CHECK: which edition of KCSIE is current. It is ordinarily updated with effect from 1 September each year, and Part 1 in particular changes. Do not cite a year in staff-facing material unless you have confirmed it; the system links to the edition configured in your settings, which must be reviewed each September.]`

### 3.5 Where we work with under-fives

3.5.1 The **Early Years Foundation Stage (EYFS) statutory framework** sets learning, development, safeguarding and welfare requirements for early years provision in England, covering children from birth to 31 August following their fifth birthday.

3.5.2 The EYFS safeguarding and welfare requirements are directly relevant to us **if we are an Ofsted-registered early years provider**. They include, among other things: a written safeguarding policy and procedures, a designated lead, staff suitability and disqualification arrangements, ratios and qualifications, paediatric first aid, and — importantly — **notification requirements to Ofsted**:

- any **serious accident, injury or serious illness to, or the death of, a child** in our care must be notified to Ofsted (and to local child protection agencies where relevant) **as soon as reasonably practicable, and in any event within 14 days** of the incident. Failing to do so without reasonable excuse is an offence.
- **food poisoning affecting two or more children** in our care is likewise notifiable.
- allegations of serious harm or abuse by any person living, working or looking after children at the premises are notifiable.

> `[CHECK: the 14-day figure and the exact list of notifiable events against the EYFS framework edition in force. The 14-day outer limit is long-standing and the drafter is confident of it, but the framework has been restructured (separate frameworks for group/school-based providers and for childminders) and the precise wording and the list of notifiable events should be read directly. Note also the separate EYFS complaints duty — investigate a written complaint about EYFS requirements and notify the outcome within 28 days, keeping the record for at least three years.]`

3.5.3 **An important exemption.** Providers of care that runs **exclusively before or after school, or during school holidays**, for children who **already attend reception class or above**, are exempt from the EYFS *learning and development* requirements. They are **not** thereby exempt from the safeguarding and welfare expectations that apply to their registration.

> `[CHECK: the precise scope of this exemption and exactly which welfare requirements continue to apply to your provision. Confirm it against the EYFS framework and, if in doubt, with Ofsted or your local authority's early years team — do not rely on this template.]`

### 3.6 Ofsted registration

## 5. Our commitments

5.1 We will:

- create a culture in which children are listened to, taken seriously and know how to raise something that is worrying them;
- recruit safely, and supervise well;
- train every adult before they work with children, and keep that training current;
- record concerns promptly, factually and confidentially, and act on them the same day;
- refer to statutory agencies without delay and without needing to be certain;
- treat information sharing as an obligation, subject to proportionality — never as a risk to be avoided;
- respond to a concern about one of our own adults with the same rigour as a concern about anyone else, and never suppress one to protect our reputation;
- work openly with parents and carers, except where doing so would place a child at greater risk;
- review this policy annually and after every significant incident.

5.2 We will **not**:

- promise a child confidentiality;
- investigate a suspicion of abuse ourselves;
- delay a referral in order to gather more evidence or to "be sure";
- allow a member of staff to work unsupervised with children before their checks are complete;
- allow a concern to be filtered, softened or dropped by a manager between the person who saw it and the DSL.

## 6. Safer recruitment and suitability

6.1 Safer recruitment is the largest single thing we do to prevent harm. It is a process, not a DBS certificate.

6.2 **The rule that governs everything in this section: no one begins unsupervised work with children until every clearance below is complete and verified.** In the system this is enforced by the **Cleared to start** gate in **Team & invites → Onboarding**. Until all gated items are satisfied the person's status reads **Start on hold**, and the outstanding items are named on the banner. A person on hold may attend induction and may shadow while directly supervised by a cleared member of staff at all times; they may not be counted in ratios, may not be alone with a child, and may not be given responsibility for a group.

### 6.3 Advertising and application

- Every advert and job description states that we are committed to safeguarding, that the role is subject to an enhanced DBS check, and that we expect all staff to share that commitment.
- We use an application form, not a CV alone, so that we get a **full employment history** and can see gaps.
- **Gaps in employment history are explored at interview and the explanation is recorded** — in the `Employment history + gaps explained` field on the onboarding record.

### 6.4 Interview

- At least two people are involved in any decision to appoint, at least one of whom has had safer recruitment training. **[PLACEHOLDER — name the trained interviewer]**
- We ask values-based and scenario questions about safeguarding, and we probe motivation for working with children.
- **Interview notes are retained** in `Safer-recruitment interview notes` on the onboarding record.

### 6.5 Identity, address and right to work

- We verify **identity** from an original photographic document (passport, photocard driving licence, BRP/eVisa or national ID card), and we take and keep a copy. Recorded under **Identity** → `ID method`, `ID document (upload)`, and confirmed by the `Identity verified` check.
- We verify **current address** from a separate, recent document — a utility bill or bank statement from the last three months, a current-year council tax bill, a current tenancy agreement or a recent HMRC/DWP letter. A passport cannot be used, because it does not show an address.
- We verify **right to work in the UK** before the first day of work, by share code (eVisa), passport, or the permitted document combinations. Recorded under **Right to work**; the `Right to work verified` check is a **gated** item.
- We keep a dated record of the check and who carried it out. Right-to-work checking is a legal obligation independent of safeguarding, and the penalty for getting it wrong falls on us.

### 6.6 DBS

6.6.1 Anyone who will teach, train, instruct, care for or supervise children **unsupervised**, and does so frequently, is in **regulated activity** and requires an **Enhanced DBS check including a check of the children's barred list**.

6.6.2 The frequency test for regulated activity is broadly: the activity is carried out **frequently** (once a week or more), or on **four or more days in any 30-day period**, or **overnight**. A person who is genuinely and always supervised to a reasonable standard by someone who is themselves in regulated activity is **not** in regulated activity — but in practice, in our provision, almost every coach and session lead is.

> `[CHECK: the frequency and overnight tests against the current DBS eligibility guidance before you apply them to a marginal role. The drafter is confident of the once-a-week / four-days-in-30 / overnight formulation, but the overnight period definition and the supervision guidance have detail that matters for volunteers and occasional helpers. Where you are unsure whether a role is eligible for an enhanced check with barred list, use the DBS eligibility tool and record the answer — over-checking is unlawful, under-checking is dangerous.]`

6.6.3 We record: `DBS certificate number`, `DBS issue date`, an upload of the certificate, and whether the person is registered with the **DBS Update Service** and their Update Service number. The **`DBS seen & cleared`** check is a **gated** item — the certificate must have been physically seen by us, not merely described.

6.6.4 **Renewal.** A DBS certificate has **no expiry date**; it is a snapshot of the day it was issued. There is no statutory renewal period for out-of-school settings.

- Our renewal interval is **[PLACEHOLDER — commonly every 3 years; state yours]**, counted from the certificate issue date. This is set once in **[PLATFORM]** and drives the expiry warning on the onboarding record.
- Where a person is on the **DBS Update Service**, we carry out a **free online status check at least annually and before any change of role**, and we record the date and result of each check.
- We do not treat "they had a DBS at their last club" as a check. We see the certificate, or we obtain our own.

6.6.5 **It is a criminal offence to knowingly engage a barred person in regulated activity.** If a certificate discloses a barred-list match, the person does not start, and we take advice immediately.

6.6.6 **Overseas checks.** Where someone has lived or worked outside the UK for **three months or more**, a UK DBS check will not cover that period. We obtain a certificate of good conduct or equivalent overseas check, or where none is available, additional overseas references and a written risk assessment of the decision to appoint. Recorded under `Overseas check (if 3+ months abroad)` — *Not needed / Requested / Received*.

6.6.7 **Disqualification.** Where we provide childcare to which the disqualification arrangements apply, we obtain a signed self-declaration that the person is not disqualified from working in childcare, and we keep the signed copy. Recorded as `Disqualification self-declaration signed` with the signed document uploaded.

> `[CHECK: whether the disqualification arrangements apply to your provision, and the exact citation. The relevant instrument is generally referred to as the Childcare (Disqualification) Regulations 2018, which among other things removed "disqualification by association" for school-based settings. Whether and how it applies to your out-of-school provision depends on your registration status (§3.6) and the ages you take. ### 6.7 References

- We take **at least two references**, one of which must be from the most recent employer or, for a first job, from an appropriate person who has known the applicant in a responsible capacity (not a friend or relative).
- References are **requested directly from the organisation**, on our own request, not accepted as an open letter carried by the applicant. Where a reference arrives from a personal email address or reads as though the applicant wrote it, we verify it by telephone.
- We ask specifically about **suitability to work with children** and about any disciplinary or safeguarding matters, and we resolve any discrepancy with the applicant before appointment.
- References are taken up **before** the person starts, not after. The `References received & satisfactory` check is a **gated** item.
- Reference details are recorded on the onboarding record under **References & history**.

### 6.8 Qualifications, first aid and training records

- We verify and keep copies of qualifications relevant to the role (coaching awards, teaching qualifications, first aid, lifeguarding). Recorded under **Qualifications & training** → `Certificates required for this role`, with uploads.
- Certificates required for a role are configured **per role**, so a coaching qualification is required of coaches and not of the office administrator.
- Where we run provision requiring **paediatric first aid**, or where the age range makes it necessary, we ensure a suitably qualified first aider is present at every session and that the qualification is in date. **[PLACEHOLDER — state your first-aid staffing standard]**
- Certificate expiry is tracked, and an out-of-date safeguarding or first-aid certificate is treated as a compliance failure, not an administrative one. The `Key certificates must be in date` setting is enabled.

### 6.9 Volunteers

- Volunteers are subject to the same identity, reference and induction requirements as paid staff.

## 8. Staff code of conduct

8.1 This section is deliberately specific. Vague expectations protect no one — not the child, and not the adult who is later accused of something they did not do. **Following it protects you as much as it protects them.**

8.2 The overarching test: **would you be comfortable if a parent, a colleague or your manager saw or heard this, and could you explain it?** If not, do not do it.

### 8.3 Physical contact

- Physical contact must be **necessary, appropriate to the activity, appropriate to the child's age and needs, and in response to the child's needs rather than the adult's**.
- It is legitimate and often necessary to make physical contact when: providing first aid; preventing an injury; supporting or spotting in a sport or gymnastics; demonstrating a technique; comforting a distressed young child in an open setting.
- Where possible, **ask, explain and gain the child's agreement first** — "I'm going to support your back here, is that OK?"
- Contact takes place **in the open, in view of others**. Never in a closed room, a store cupboard, a vehicle or an unobserved corner.
- **Never**: contact for the adult's benefit; contact of a sexual nature; play-fighting, tickling or rough games; carrying, cuddling or having a child sit on your lap (except where a very young child is distressed and it is done openly and briefly); touching a child in a way you would not do in front of their parent.
- Some children — those who have been abused, those with certain SEND, those from particular cultural backgrounds — may find contact distressing or may seek inappropriate contact. Where a child needs a specific approach, it is agreed with the parent, written down, and shared with the staff who need it.
- If contact is unavoidable and could be misread, or if a child reacts unexpectedly, **report it to the DSL yourself the same day and record it**. Self-reporting is expected and is never held against you.

### 8.4 One-to-one working

- **We avoid one-to-one working with a child wherever it can be avoided.**
- Where it is unavoidable (individual coaching, tutoring, a child needing a quiet conversation, intimate care), it takes place:
  - in a room with a **window or an open door**, or in an open area;
  - in a location where **another adult knows you are there and could walk in**;
  - having told a colleague or manager **beforehand** where you will be, with whom, and for how long;
  - with a **record made afterwards** in the daily log — time, place, who, why.
- One-to-one **tuition or coaching as a service** is arranged so that a parent or a second adult is present or immediately adjacent. **[PLACEHOLDER — state your rule; a blanket "a second adult is always present" is the safest and is what most providers should adopt]**
- We do not arrange to meet a child alone away from our provision, at our home, or at theirs.

### 8.5 Behaviour, physical intervention and restraint

- We manage behaviour positively. See our Behaviour Management Policy. **[PLACEHOLDER — cross-refer]**
- **Physical intervention is a last resort**, used only to prevent a child from injuring themselves or others, causing significant damage, or committing a criminal offence. It must be reasonable, proportionate and the minimum necessary.
- Only staff trained and authorised under an accredited scheme may use any planned physical intervention. An in-house briefing is not training. Where we authorise physical intervention at all, the training meets the recognised UK standard for restraint reduction. **[PLACEHOLDER — state whether you authorise physical intervention at all; many providers should state plainly that they do not, other than to prevent immediate harm]**
- **Every instance of physical intervention is recorded the same day** in **Log a concern → Behaviour**, and the parent is informed the same day.
- We never use physical punishment, force-feeding, deprivation of food or drink, humiliation, isolation in a locked space, or any form of degrading treatment. Corporal punishment is unlawful.

### 8.6 Transport

- We do not transport a child in a private vehicle **except** where it has been agreed in advance in writing by the parent and a manager, and recorded. **[PLACEHOLDER — many providers should state simply "We do not transport children in staff private vehicles under any circumstances"]**
- Where transport is provided, there is **never a single adult alone with a single child** in a vehicle. Two adults, or more than one child, or the journey does not happen.
- Where an emergency makes a lone journey unavoidable, the member of staff informs a manager **before** setting off, the child sits in the back, and the journey and reason are recorded on return.
- Drivers hold a valid licence, appropriate business insurance and, where required, the correct category and any necessary DBS clearance; vehicles are roadworthy and correctly seated for the children's ages and sizes.
- On minibus or coach travel: a headcount on and off, a named adult responsible for each group, and the register carried.

### 8.7 Personal mobile phones and devices

- **Personal phones are not used during contact time with children.** They are kept in a bag or a designated place, on silent.
- **Personal devices are never used to photograph or film children.** Ever. Not "just this once", not "I'll delete it after", not for a parent who asked.
- Personal phones are not taken into changing rooms, toilets or any area where children change, under any circumstances.
- Where staff need a phone for emergency communication on a trip or an off-site session, the setting's designated phone is used, or the arrangement is agreed in advance with a manager and recorded.
- Staff make and take personal calls in breaks, away from children.
- **[PLACEHOLDER — state the sanction; for most providers, photographing a child on a personal device is a disciplinary matter capable of amounting to gross misconduct]**

### 8.8 Photography and images

- Photographs and video of children are taken **only on setting-owned devices or accounts**, only by staff authorised to do so, and only for a purpose we have told parents about.
- **We check consent before we take, use, tag or share an image.** Photo consent is asked at booking and is held on the child's record, and staff can see it at a glance on the child's card in the **Register** (📷 photos yes/no).
- In **Moments**, a child **without photo consent cannot be tagged** in a shareable post — the system blocks it and shows a red *no photos* flag next to their name. Staff do not work around this by cropping, by describing the child, or by posting to a different channel.
- Children are appropriately dressed in any image. We do not use a child's full name alongside their image publicly. We do not publish images that identify a child's location and routine together.
- Images are stored on setting systems, not on personal devices or personal cloud accounts, and are deleted in line with §23.
- **A parent's consent can be withdrawn at any time.** When it is, we stop using the image going forward and remove it from anything we control.
- Parents photographing at our events: we set the rule at the start of the event — **[PLACEHOLDER — e.g. "parents may photograph their own child only; no posting of images containing other children"]** — and we honour any family's request not to be photographed.
- External photographers are DBS-checked where required, are never left alone with children, and work to a written brief.

### 8.9 Social media and contact outside sessions

- **Staff do not accept, initiate or maintain "friend", "follow" or direct-message contact with a child who attends our provision, on any personal account.** This includes gaming platforms and messaging apps.
- Staff do not give children their personal phone number, personal email address or personal social media handles.
- All communication with a child or a family goes through **setting channels** — the **Messages** area, the setting email address, or the setting phone. This is not bureaucracy: it means there is a record, and it removes the private space in which grooming happens.
- Where a child or a young person attempts to make personal contact, the member of staff does not respond, and **reports it to the DSL the same day** so that it can be recorded and handled openly.
- Where a member of staff has a genuine pre-existing relationship with a family (a relative, a family friend, a neighbour), they declare it in writing at the point it arises, and a manager records how any conflict will be handled.
- Staff keep their personal social media private and do not post content that would undermine confidence in their suitability to work with children. We do not police private lives, but public conduct that a parent could reasonably link to us is our business.
- Staff do not discuss children, families, incidents or colleagues on social media, in any form, however anonymised they believe it to be.

### 8.10 Position of trust

Every adult working for us is in a position of trust in relation to every child in our provision. **Sexual activity with a 16- or 17-year-old in our provision is a criminal offence** notwithstanding the age of consent (§3.2). Any romantic or sexual relationship, or its pursuit, between a member of staff and any child at our provision, is prohibited absolutely and will be treated as a safeguarding matter under §13, not merely a disciplinary one.

### 8.11 Gifts, favouritism and boundaries

- Staff do not give personal gifts to individual children or receive significant gifts from them or their families.
- Staff do not favour, single out, or form an exclusive relationship with a particular child, or offer lifts, treats, private time or special access.
- Staff do not offer to babysit, tutor privately, or otherwise take on a private paid or unpaid caring role for a family they met through us without declaring it in writing in advance. **[PLACEHOLDER — state whether you permit this at all; a written prohibition is cleaner and many providers adopt one]**

### 8.12 Alcohol, drugs and fitness to work

Staff do not consume alcohol or non-prescribed drugs before or during work with children, and do not attend work impaired. Staff taking prescribed medication that could affect their capacity inform a manager. Smoking and vaping do not take place in the presence of children.

### 8.13 Intimate care and toileting

- Intimate care (toileting, nappy changing, changing soiled clothing, personal care for a disabled child) is provided only where it is part of our agreed provision and with a **written care plan agreed with the parent**.
- It is carried out by a named, cleared member of staff, in a manner that preserves dignity and privacy, **with another adult aware and nearby**, and it is logged each time.
- Children are encouraged to do as much for themselves as they can.
- Staff do not take a child to the toilet alone behind a closed door where this can be avoided; where a very young child needs assistance, the door is left ajar or a second adult is nearby.
- Any concern arising during intimate care — a mark, something the child says, a child's reaction — is reported to the DSL the same day.

### 8.14 Changing rooms

Adults do not change or shower in the presence of children, and children are supervised in a way that respects privacy — supervision from outside or at the entrance, with staff entering only where necessary and announcing themselves. No devices in changing areas, for staff or children.

## 9. Recognising abuse and neglect

9.1 **You do not need to be sure. You need to notice and pass it on.** It is not your job to decide whether abuse has occurred, to weigh the evidence, or to rule anything out. If something worries you, report it.

9.2 Abuse and neglect are forms of maltreatment. Somebody may abuse or neglect a child by inflicting harm, or by failing to act to prevent harm. Abuse can be perpetrated by an adult or adults, or by another child or children. It can happen in a family, in an institution, in a community setting, or wholly online.

### 9.3 The four categories

**Physical abuse** — hitting, shaking, throwing, poisoning, burning or scalding, drowning, suffocating, or otherwise causing physical harm. It also includes a parent or carer fabricating or deliberately inducing illness in a child.

*What you might see:* bruises, burns, bite marks, fractures or scalds, particularly in unusual places (back, buttocks, upper arms, thighs, ears, neck), in clusters, or in the shape of an object; injuries at different stages of healing; an explanation that does not fit the injury, changes each time it is given, or is offered too readily; a child who flinches at sudden movement; refusal to change for an activity or to remove outer clothing in hot weather; frequent absences after injuries.

*Bruising in a baby or a child who is not yet independently mobile is a serious concern and always requires a same-day referral.*

**Emotional abuse** — persistent emotional maltreatment causing severe and lasting adverse effects on a child's emotional development. Conveying that a child is worthless, unloved, inadequate, or valued only insofar as they meet someone else's needs. Age- or developmentally inappropriate expectations. Overprotection and limitation of exploration and learning, or preventing participation in normal social interaction. Seeing or hearing the ill-treatment of another. Serious bullying, including online. Causing a child frequently to feel frightened or in danger. Some level of emotional abuse is involved in all types of maltreatment, though it can occur alone.

*What you might see:* excessive fear of making a mistake; developmental delay; extremes of passivity or aggression; a child who is unusually watchful of adults; self-deprecating language ("I'm rubbish", "I'm stupid"); a parent who consistently belittles, blames, scapegoats or ignores the child; a child who does not seek comfort when distressed.

**Sexual abuse** — forcing or enticing a child to take part in sexual activities, whether or not the child is aware of what is happening, and whether or not violence is involved. It includes physical contact (penetrative and non-penetrative acts) and non-contact activities: involving children in looking at or in the production of sexual images, watching sexual activities, encouraging children to behave in sexually inappropriate ways, or grooming a child in preparation for abuse, including online. **Women and other children can commit acts of sexual abuse, as well as men.**

*What you might see:* sexual knowledge, language or behaviour inconsistent with the child's age and stage; sexualised play involving other children; pain, itching, bleeding or bruising in the genital area; discomfort walking or sitting; recurrent urinary infections; a child who becomes withdrawn, self-harming, or develops an eating disorder; a strong reluctance to be with a particular person; unexplained gifts, money or possessions; a much older "boyfriend" or "girlfriend".

**Neglect** — the persistent failure to meet a child's basic physical and/or psychological needs, likely to result in serious impairment of the child's health or development. It may involve a parent or carer failing to provide adequate food, clothing and shelter; to protect a child from physical and emotional harm or danger; to ensure adequate supervision (including the use of inadequate care-givers); or to ensure access to appropriate medical care or treatment. It also includes neglect of, or unresponsiveness to, a child's basic emotional needs. Neglect may occur during pregnancy.

*What you might see:* persistently unwashed, smelly or inadequately dressed for the weather; untreated medical or dental problems; constant hunger, food-seeking, hoarding or stealing food; extreme tiredness; frequent lateness or non-attendance; being left alone or in the care of unsuitable people; a parent who is repeatedly late or unreachable at collection; poor or worsening school attendance reported by the family; a child taking on adult responsibility for siblings.

9.4 **Neglect is the hardest to spot and the most commonly under-reported**, precisely because it is cumulative rather than dramatic. One grubby jumper is nothing. The same child, cold and hungry, in the same jumper, across a season, is a pattern — and the only way we will see the pattern is if each individual small thing was written down at the time. **Record small things. That is how neglect is found.**

9.5 Behaviour is communication. A child who is aggressive, disruptive, withdrawn, obsessively compliant, regressing, or suddenly changed is telling us something. Where a child's behaviour changes markedly, we record it in **Log a concern → Behaviour** and we consider whether there is a safeguarding explanation before we treat it as a discipline matter.

9.6 The absence of an indicator proves nothing, and the presence of one proves nothing. Children who are abused very often present as entirely ordinary. Trust what you notice; report it; let the DSL decide.

## 10. Specific risks we plan for

### 10.1 Child-on-child abuse

10.1.1 Children can and do abuse other children. It happens in our provision as it happens everywhere children are together. **We never dismiss it as "banter", "having a laugh", "part of growing up", "just messing about" or "boys being boys".** Doing so normalises it, silences the child harmed, and leaves the child causing harm without help.

10.1.2 It includes: bullying (including cyberbullying, prejudice-based and discriminatory bullying); abuse in intimate relationships between young people; physical abuse; sexual violence and sexual harassment; consensual and non-consensual sharing of nude and semi-nude images ("nudes"); causing someone to engage in sexual activity without consent; upskirting; and initiation or "hazing" rituals.

10.1.3 We respond to **both** children — the one harmed and the one causing harm. A child who abuses another child may well be being abused themselves, or may be re-enacting what they have seen. Both need a safeguarding response, not only a disciplinary one.

10.1.4 Every allegation is recorded in **Log a concern → Safeguarding** under **Child-on-child abuse (bullying, harassment)** or, where sexual, under **Sexual abuse / harmful sexual behaviour**, and goes to the DSL the same day. Sexual violence and sexual harassment between children are **never** handled as a behaviour matter alone.

10.1.5 We take practical steps to reduce risk: known supervision of toilets and changing rooms, no unsupervised areas, awareness of which children should be kept apart, and adults who are visibly present rather than clustered together at the side.

10.1.6 **Nudes and semi-nudes.** Staff do **not** view, copy, forward, print, or ask a child to show them an image. Staff do not search a child's phone. They report to the DSL immediately, and the DSL follows the national guidance for responding to sexual imagery shared by children. Where a young person wants an image taken down, we can point them to Report Remove (NSPCC / Internet Watch Foundation), while being honest that removal everywhere cannot be guaranteed.

It is written for education settings; confirm you are applying an appropriate version for an out-of-school setting.]`

### 10.2 Bullying

Recorded, addressed, and reviewed. Persistent bullying is a safeguarding matter, not only a behaviour one, and is escalated to the DSL. See our Behaviour Management Policy. **[PLACEHOLDER — cross-refer, and state your anti-bullying approach]**

### 10.3 Child sexual exploitation (CSE)

A form of sexual abuse in which a child is manipulated or coerced into sexual activity in exchange for something they need or want — money, gifts, drugs, alcohol, accommodation, affection, status or protection — or for the financial or status gain of the perpetrator. It can occur without physical contact and entirely online. **A child cannot consent to their own exploitation**, and apparent consent is irrelevant.

*Signs:* unexplained money, phones, clothes or gifts; an older or controlling "partner"; going missing, or returning late and unwilling to say where they have been; secretive phone use, multiple phones or SIMs; sudden changes in friendship group; disengagement from activities they used to love; being picked up by unknown adults.

### 10.4 Child criminal exploitation and county lines

Children coerced or manipulated into criminal activity, including transporting and selling drugs across areas ("county lines"), moving money or weapons, or shoplifting to order. Children involved are **victims**, even when they appear willing and even when they have committed offences.

*Signs:* unexplained travel, particularly by train; unexplained money or multiple phones; going missing from home or provision; carrying weapons; new and unexplained injuries; being fearful of certain people or areas; sudden self-imposed isolation.

Recorded under **Exploitation (CSE / CCE) or modern slavery**. Where there are indicators of modern slavery or trafficking, the DSL considers a **National Referral Mechanism (NRM)** referral alongside referral to children's social care and the police, and records the decision in the DSL action log.

### 10.5 Contextual safeguarding

Much of the risk to older children comes from **outside the home** — peer groups, neighbourhoods, transport, online spaces. When we assess risk we consider the contexts a child moves through, not only their family, and we share what we know with the local authority so that a fuller picture can be built.

### 10.6 Female genital mutilation (FGM)

10.6.1 FGM is a criminal offence and a form of child abuse. It carries no religious requirement and no medical justification.

10.6.2 **Mandatory reporting — read this carefully.** There is a **personal, statutory duty to report FGM directly to the police** where a girl under 18 discloses that she has undergone FGM, or where visual evidence of FGM is observed. That personal duty falls on **specified regulated professionals — health and social care professionals and teachers**. It does **not**, as a general rule, fall on a sports coach, a play worker or a camp leader.

### 10.8 Domestic abuse

Signs of self-harm, disordered eating, or expressions of hopelessness or suicidal thinking are recorded under **Mental health (self-harm, suicidal ideation)** and go to the DSL the same day. **If there is an immediate risk to life, call 999 first, then tell the DSL.** Where mental health difficulties may be a consequence of abuse or trauma, it is a safeguarding matter.

### 10.11 Online harm arising outside our sessions

Where a child tells us about, or we observe, harm arising online — grooming, sextortion, harassment, exposure to harmful content, contact from an unknown adult — it is recorded under **Online / digital harm** and treated exactly as any other safeguarding concern. Staff do not investigate, do not view images, and do not contact the perpetrator. See §19 and §20.

### 10.12 Allergies, medical needs and life-threatening conditions

Failing to manage a known allergy or medical condition is a safeguarding failure, not an administrative one. Allergies, medical needs, SEND and continence needs are held on the child's record and appear **permanently on the child's card in the Register** — not hidden behind a tab — so that they cannot be missed. Where a child carries an adrenaline auto-injector or emergency medication, every member of staff on that session is told at the arrival briefing where it is and who is trained to use it. See §21.

## 11. If a child tells you something — what to do

11.1 **This is the most important page in this policy.** A child who has decided to tell you may have taken months to get to that moment, and may not try again.

### 11.2 In the moment

**DO:**

1. **Stay calm.** Your reaction is what the child will remember. Shock, anger or distress on your face may stop them talking.
2. **Listen.** Give them your full attention. Let them say it in their own way, at their own pace.
3. **Reassure them that they have done the right thing by telling you**, and that it is not their fault.
4. **Use open, non-leading prompts only** — "Tell me more about that", "And then what happened?", "Is there anything else you want to tell me?", "Can you help me understand?" (the TED prompts: **T**ell me, **E**xplain to me, **D**escribe to me).
5. **Explain, honestly and simply, what you are going to do next** — at a level they can understand. "What you've told me is important, and I need to tell [DSL name], because it's their job to help keep you safe. I'll tell you what happens next."
6. **Write it down as soon as possible afterwards** — the same day, ideally within the hour, while it is fresh.
7. **Tell the DSL the same day.** Immediately if the child may be in danger.

**DO NOT:**

1. **Do not promise confidentiality.** Never say "I won't tell anyone" or "this is just between us". If a child asks you to promise, say: *"I can't promise to keep it a secret, because if someone is hurting you, my job is to help keep you safe. But I promise I'll only tell the people who need to know, and I'll tell you what I'm doing."* Say this **before** they tell you if you can — but never at the cost of shutting them down.
2. **Do not ask leading or closed questions.** Not "Did he touch you?", "Was it your dad?", "Did that happen in the bedroom?". Leading questions can contaminate evidence and can result in a prosecution failing. Let the child use their own words.
3. **Do not press for detail, and do not ask them to repeat it.** You need enough to pass on, not a full account. Repeated retelling is distressing and damaging.
4. **Do not investigate.** Do not speak to the alleged perpetrator, the parents, other children, or other witnesses. That is for the statutory agencies.
5. **Do not react with disgust, disbelief or judgement**, about the child, the family or the person named.
6. **Do not say "why didn't you tell someone before?"** — it reads as blame.
7. **Do not delay.** Not until the end of the session, not until Monday, not until you have thought about it, not until you feel more certain.
8. **Do not tell colleagues, friends or family.** Not in the staff room, not in the car park, not anonymised.
9. **Do not take photographs of injuries** on any device. Use the **body map** on the concern form to mark and describe what you saw.
10. **Do not remove or handle clothing or physical items** that may be evidence.

### 11.3 Recording it

11.3.1 Record **the same day, and as soon as you reasonably can**, in **Log a concern → Safeguarding**. Every record is automatically timestamped and attributed to you.

11.3.2 What to record:

- **The child's own words, verbatim, in quotation marks.** Not your summary, not tidied-up grammar, not adult vocabulary substituted for theirs. If a child says a word you would not use, write that word. The exact words matter.
- **What you saw** — appearance, demeanour, injuries, marks. Use the **body map** to number and locate each mark, with a note against each.
- **What you asked**, exactly, and in what order — so it is clear you did not lead.
- **When and where** it happened, and when and where the child told you.
- **Who else was present**, recorded in **Witnesses**.
- **What you did immediately** — recorded in **Immediate action taken**.
- **Who you told, and when.**

11.3.3 Record **facts, not opinion, interpretation or speculation.** "Jamie said 'my mum's boyfriend hit me with the belt'. There was a red mark about 10cm long across the back of his left thigh" — not "Jamie seems to be being abused at home, I think the mum's partner is violent". Where you do form a professional judgement, mark it clearly as such and separate it from what you observed.

11.3.4 Do not delay the record because a field is blank or you are unsure. **An incomplete record made today beats a perfect record made on Monday.**

11.3.5 The record is **confidential by default**. Safeguarding concerns are not visible to parents and are not shared outside the DSL route unless the DSL decides otherwise.

11.3.6 If the system is unavailable, **write it on paper immediately**, sign and date it, hand it to the DSL, and transcribe it into the system as soon as you can. Keep the original paper note — it is the contemporaneous record.

### 11.4 After you have passed it on

- **Your responsibility does not end when you press save.** If you do not hear that the concern has been picked up, **ask**. If you are not satisfied with the response, escalate (§14).
- Look after yourself. Receiving a disclosure is hard. Support is available from **[PLACEHOLDER — named person / EAP / supervision arrangement]**, and you may talk to the DSL about how you are, as long as you do not discuss the case with anyone else.
- Do not treat the child differently afterwards. Consistency, warmth and normality are among the most protective things we can offer.

### 11.5 If a child tells you about another child

The same rules apply. Both children are recorded, and both are considered under §10.1.

### 11.6 If a child tells you about a member of staff

Follow §11.2 exactly, then go **immediately** to the case manager (§4.4) — not to the person named, not to their line manager if that is the person named. See §13.

## 12. Reporting and referral

### 12.1 The rule

**Any concern, however small, goes to the DSL — the same day.**

There is no threshold you must reach before reporting internally, no minimum severity, and no such thing as wasting the DSL's time. If a child is in **immediate danger**, act first (999 or an ambulance), then report.

### 12.2 How you report internally

1. Open **Log a concern → Safeguarding** and record it (§11.3). The DSL is alerted automatically on every concern.
2. **Also tell the DSL directly** — in person, by phone, or by message — where the concern is serious or urgent. **Do not rely on a notification alone for anything urgent.** A system alert is a record, not a guarantee that a human has read it in the next ten minutes.
3. If you cannot reach the DSL, contact the deputy. If you can reach neither and a child may be at risk of significant harm, go straight to children's social care or the police yourself (§12.10) and tell the DSL as soon as you can.

### 12.4 Deciding to refer

12.4.1 The DSL refers to children's social care where a child **is in need of services**, or where there is **reasonable cause to suspect that a child is suffering, or is likely to suffer, significant harm**. The DSL does not need proof, does not need to have ruled out innocent explanations, and does not need the family's agreement.

12.4.2 **If in doubt, take advice.** The DSL can ring the local authority's duty/consultation line, or the NSPCC helpline, to discuss a concern anonymously before deciding. A consultation is not a referral, but it is recorded.

12.4.3 **We do not wait to be sure.** Waiting for certainty is how children stay in harm.

- prejudice a police investigation;
- lead to the child being removed from the country (see §10.6 on FGM and honour-based abuse).

12.8.3 Within our organisation, safeguarding information is shared strictly on a **need-to-know** basis. 12.8.4 Where a child moves to another provider or a new school and we hold an open safeguarding concern, the DSL considers whether information should be passed on, and records the decision.

## 13. Allegations and concerns about staff, volunteers and others working with children

13.1 This is a **separate procedure** from a concern about a child, and it is a procedure we follow even when — especially when — we do not believe the allegation.

13.2 It applies to any allegation or concern that a person who works with our children has:

- behaved in a way that has harmed, or may have harmed, a child;
- possibly committed a criminal offence against, or related to, a child;
- behaved towards a child in a way that indicates they may pose a risk of harm to children;
- behaved or may have behaved in a way that indicates they may not be suitable to work with children.

13.3 It applies to employees, casual staff, volunteers, contractors, agency staff, students, visitors, and anyone else engaged by us — **including the DSL, including managers, and including the proprietor**.

13.4 It applies to conduct **outside** work, and to conduct in a person's **personal life**, where it bears on their suitability to work with children — including allegations arising from their own family life or another organisation.

### 13.5 If you receive or witness one

1. **Take it seriously.** Note exactly what was said or seen.
2. **Report it to the case manager immediately** (§4.4). Same day is not good enough if the person is still on shift with children — it is **now**.
3. If the allegation concerns the case manager, report instead to **[PLACEHOLDER — the alternative named person in §4.4]**, or go **directly to the LADO**.
4. **Do not investigate.** Do not question the child further, beyond what is needed to be clear what is being alleged. Do not interview witnesses. Do not gather evidence.
5. **Do not speak to the person concerned about it, or tip them off in any way.** Not "there's something I should mention", not a warning, not a hint. Tipping off can destroy evidence and can amount to an offence in itself.
6. **Do not discuss it with colleagues.**
7. **Make the child safe now** — separate the child from the person, discreetly and without drama.
8. Record it in **Log a concern → Safeguarding**, selecting **A member of staff** as the subject. The form switches to the allegations process and shows the case-manager route rather than the ordinary DSL route.

### 13.6 What the case manager does

1. **Immediately** ensures the child is safe and that the person has no further contact with children pending a decision.
2. Obtains **written details of the allegation**, signed and dated by the person who received it — a factual record only, no investigation.
3. Makes an **initial assessment** against the four criteria in §13.2, without questioning the child or the person.
4. **Where the harm threshold may be met, refers to the LADO within one working day.** The case manager does **not** decide alone that the threshold is not met if there is any doubt — they consult the LADO. Consultation is free and is what the LADO is there for.
5. Follows the LADO's advice on next steps, including on whether and when the person may be told, and on any police involvement. **We do not tell the person until we have been advised it is safe to do so.**
6. Where the allegation may be a criminal offence, refers to the **police**, and does not take any step that could prejudice an investigation.
7. Considers whether a **referral to children's social care** about the child is also needed — the two run in parallel.
8. Where we are Ofsted-registered, considers the **separate notification duty to Ofsted** (§3.5.2).
9. Where we work on a school site or under a school contract, informs the **school's DSL / headteacher** as agreed (§24).
10. Keeps a **full, contemporaneous, chronological record** of every decision, conversation and action, with dates and times.
11. Keeps the person informed of progress so far as the LADO and the police permit, and ensures they have appropriate support and a named point of contact.
12. Keeps the child, and the family, appropriately informed and supported.

### 13.7 Suspension

13.7.1 **Suspension is not automatic and is not a neutral act.** It is damaging to the individual and should not be the default.

13.7.2 It is considered only where there is **cause to suspect a child is at risk of significant harm**, or the allegation is so serious that it might be grounds for dismissal, or suspension is necessary to allow a fair investigation.

13.7.3 **Alternatives are considered and recorded first**, for example: redeployment to a role with no unsupervised child contact; working under direct supervision at all times; a change of duties, site or hours; a temporary move away from a particular child or group.

13.7.4 Where we are considering suspension, we take the LADO's view first wherever it is possible to do so, and we record the reasoning either way. Suspension is a decision for **[PLACEHOLDER — named role]**, is confirmed in writing, and is reviewed regularly rather than left to run.

### 13.8 Low-level concerns

13.8.1 A **low-level concern** is any concern — however small, and even if no more than a "niggle" — that an adult working with children may have acted in a way that is inconsistent with the code of conduct, but which does not meet the harm threshold for a LADO referral.

13.8.2 Examples: being over-friendly with a child; having favourites; taking photographs of children on a personal phone; using inappropriate language; humiliating a child; being alone with a child in a closed room when it could have been avoided; giving a child a lift; adding a child on social media.

13.8.3 **We actively encourage low-level concerns to be shared.** This is not tale-telling. Serious abuse in organisations has, again and again, been preceded by small, noticed, unreported boundary-pushing that nobody wrote down. **The pattern is only visible if the individual pieces exist.**

13.8.4 Low-level concerns are reported to **[PLACEHOLDER — normally the case manager or the DSL]**, are **recorded in writing** with the date, the detail and the context, and are **retained** so that patterns can be seen over time.

13.8.5 They are reviewed periodically. Where a pattern emerges, the accumulated record is reassessed against the §13.2 criteria and may become a LADO referral.

13.8.6 An adult may also **self-report** a low-level concern about their own conduct (§8.16). Self-reporting is welcomed and is recorded in the same way.

13.8.7 A record of a low-level concern is **not** ordinarily referred to in a reference unless it forms part of a pattern relevant to suitability, or has been escalated. **[PLACEHOLDER — state your position and be consistent with it]**

### 13.9 Unsubstantiated, unfounded, false and malicious allegations

13.9.1 We use the standard outcome terminology, and we record which applies:

- **Substantiated** — there is sufficient evidence to prove the allegation.
- **Malicious** — there is sufficient evidence to disprove the allegation **and** there was a deliberate act to deceive.
- **False** — there is sufficient evidence to disprove the allegation.
- **Unsubstantiated** — there is insufficient evidence either to prove or to disprove it; this is **not** the same as a false allegation.
- **Unfounded** — there is no evidence or proper basis supporting the allegation.

13.9.3 Where an allegation is **unsubstantiated or unfounded**, the person is supported back to work properly, and we take steps to manage the impact on their reputation and wellbeing.

## 16. Missing child

16.1 **A child is missing the moment you cannot account for them. Not five minutes later.**

16.2 There is no threshold to reach, no need to be embarrassed, and no penalty for raising the alarm and finding the child in the toilet thirty seconds later. **Raising the alarm quickly and being wrong is exactly what we want.**

### 16.3 Immediate actions

1. **Alert the session lead immediately** and say clearly: the child's name, where and when they were last seen, and what they are wearing.
2. The session lead **stops the session** and takes a **register-based roll call** of all remaining children, with children gathered in one supervised place.
3. **Enough staff stay with the remaining children to maintain ratios and keep them calm and safe.** The remaining children are not left in order to search.
4. Named staff **search a defined area systematically** — the last known location first, then toilets, changing rooms, stores, vehicles, the perimeter, the exits, and any place a child might hide or be trapped. Nobody searches an area twice while another area is unsearched.
5. The venue is asked to secure or monitor exits, and the site's own staff are told.
6. If the child is not found within **[PLACEHOLDER — recommended: 10 minutes, or immediately for a very young child, a child with additional needs, a child near water or a road, or where abduction is suspected]**, the session lead **calls 999** and asks for the police, and calls the parent.
7. **The DSL is informed immediately** — in parallel with, not instead of, the above.
8. One named person coordinates and holds the information; one named person deals with the police and the parent. Staff do not all ring the parent.

### 16.4 On an off-site trip or visit

The same procedure applies, with the additional steps: an immediate head count; a designated meeting point agreed and told to children in advance; children told at the start what to do and whom to approach if they become separated; the venue's own missing-child procedure activated; and the site management, transport provider and any host organisation informed.

### 16.5 Afterwards

- Ensure the child is safe, warm, checked over and reassured. **They are not told off.** Find out gently what happened.
- Inform the parent fully and honestly, in person where possible.
- **Record it the same day** in **Log a concern**, with a full chronology: when the child was last seen, when the absence was noticed, every action and every time.
- The DSL and the senior accountable person carry out a review: how did it happen, what allowed it, what changes now. The review is written down.
- Where we are Ofsted-registered, consider whether it is a notifiable event (§3.5.2).
- Consider whether the incident indicates a wider safeguarding concern about the child (children who repeatedly abscond may be telling us something) and whether a referral is warranted.

## 20. Online sessions delivered by live video

20.1 **This section is not a formality.** Delivering tuition, coaching or activities to a child by live video puts an adult into a child's bedroom, unobserved, with a persistent private channel between them. It removes almost every environmental safeguard we rely on in a hall — other adults, sightlines, incidental supervision, a door. It therefore needs its own controls, and they need to be enforced by configuration, not by good intentions.

20.2 This section applies to any session we deliver where the venue is set to **online** — the platform treats an online venue as a first-class venue kind, with joining instructions in place of an address — and to any live video contact with a child arising from our provision, including a catch-up, a make-up lesson, a parents' meeting a child attends, or a one-off arranged by a coach.

20.3 **A session run over video is a session.** Every other part of this policy applies to it: the code of conduct, the register, the concern route, the record-keeping, the DSL. It is not an informal favour outside the policy because it happens on a laptop.

   - a **second cleared adult from our organisation present in the session** for its full duration; **or**
   - the session is **recorded in full** under §20.8, with the parent's prior written consent, and the recording retained under §20.9.

21.1 These are covered fully in our Health & Safety Policy, First Aid Policy and Medication Policy. **[PLACEHOLDER — cross-refer]** This section covers only where they touch safeguarding.

### 21.2 Accidents and first aid

- Every injury, however minor, is recorded in **First aid** on the day it happens, with the injury, the body part, the treatment given, and the first aider's name. A **body map** is used to mark the location of any injury.
- **The parent is informed the same day** and receives the record in their own area, with a timestamp. Where enabled, we ask the parent to acknowledge that they have seen it, and we chase an outstanding acknowledgement.
- **An accident record and a safeguarding concern are different things, and one does not replace the other.** Where an injury's explanation does not fit, where injuries recur, or where a child says something about how they were hurt, a **separate safeguarding concern** is raised in addition to the accident record.
- Serious accidents are reviewed for cause, and where we are Ofsted-registered we consider the notification duty (§3.5.2).

> `[CHECK: your RIDDOR position. Under the Reporting of Injuries, Diseases and Dangerous Occurrences Regulations 2013, certain work-related accidents involving a non-worker — including where a person not at work is taken directly from the scene to hospital for treatment as a result of a work-related accident — are reportable to the HSE, alongside specified injuries and deaths. Confirm the current triggers and timescales from the HSE, and name here who is responsible for making a RIDDOR report.]`

### 21.3 Medication

- **Nothing is administered without the parent's written consent.** The system enforces this: a dose cannot be recorded against a medicine that has no consent on file, and it blocks with *"Consent needed before a dose can be recorded"*.
- The parent authorises each medicine in advance, giving the medicine, the dose, when it should be given, storage, expiry and instructions, and signing a consent statement. Consent may be **withdrawn at any time**, and staff see a clear warning where it has been.
- Every dose is recorded on the **medication administration record** — what was given, the dose actually given, the date and time, who gave it and, where required, **who witnessed it**. A dose refused or missed is recorded too, as **not given**.
- Where our policy requires a **second person to witness each dose**, or restricts dose recording to **leads only**, the system enforces it and will not save without a witness.
- Medicines are stored securely, in date, and out of reach; controlled and emergency medicines according to their own arrangements. Emergency medication (adrenaline auto-injectors, inhalers, epilepsy rescue medication) is **immediately accessible**, not locked away where it cannot be reached in time.
- The parent is notified when a dose is given, and when one is missed or refused.
- **Repeated failure to supply a child's essential medication, or to collect it, is a welfare concern** and is raised with the DSL, not treated as an administrative nuisance.
- Errors — a wrong dose, a missed dose, a dose given to the wrong child — are recorded honestly, the parent is told the same day, medical advice is sought where needed, and the cause is reviewed. **We do not have a culture in which a medication error is hidden.**

### 21.4 Allergies and dietary needs

Allergies, dietary requirements and medical needs are held on the child's record and shown **permanently and prominently on the child's register card**, not tucked behind a tab. Every member of staff working with a child knows their allergies before the session starts. Where we serve food, allergen information is accurate and available. **[PLACEHOLDER — cross-refer to your allergen procedure]**

### 21.5 Intimate care and continence

See §8.13. Continence needs are recorded on the child's record, nappy and toileting changes are logged with the time and the member of staff, and any concern arising is reported the same day.

### 21.6 Trips, visits and off-site activity

- Every trip has a **written risk assessment**, appropriate ratios, a named leader, a first aider, emergency contact details carried, and a register carried and used.

## 23. Records, retention and data protection

23.1 **If it is not written down, it did not happen.** Safeguarding records are the evidence that we noticed, that we acted, and that we acted in time. They are also, sometimes years later, the material on which a criminal investigation or a public inquiry turns.

### 23.2 How we record

- **Contemporaneously** — the same day, as soon as reasonably possible.
- **Factually** — what was seen, heard and done. Opinion is separated and labelled as such.
- **In the child's own words** where they spoke, verbatim and in quotation marks.
- **Signed, dated and timed**, with the recorder identified. The system does this automatically.
- **Completely** — including the decision made, the reason for it, who was contacted, what they said, and what happens next.
- **A record is never altered after the fact.** Where something is added or corrected, it is added as a new entry with its own date and time, and the original stands. The system keeps an updated stamp and an action log rather than overwriting.

### 23.3 Data protection

23.3.1 We process safeguarding information under the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018. Safeguarding information about a child is **special category** data and is handled with the corresponding care.

> `[CHECK: the lawful basis and special category condition you rely on. The Data Protection Act 2018 contains a substantial-public-interest condition specifically covering the safeguarding of children and of individuals at risk, and it is the condition most out-of-school providers should be relying on — but you must identify it correctly in your record of processing and, where required, have an appropriate policy document in place. Have your data protection arrangements reviewed by someone competent; this is not something to lift from a template.]`

23.3.2 **A parent's right of access is not absolute.** A subject access request does not entitle a parent to a safeguarding record where disclosure would be likely to cause serious harm, would identify a third party who has not consented, or would prejudice the prevention or detection of crime. **All safeguarding-related subject access requests are referred to the DSL and to [PLACEHOLDER — your data protection contact] before anything is disclosed. Nobody else releases a safeguarding record to anyone.**

23.3.3 A request to delete data does not override a legal obligation or a safeguarding need to retain it. Our privacy information tells families this plainly: records a provider is legally required to keep may be retained for the required period.

### 23.4 Security

- Safeguarding records are held in the system, access-restricted by role. The concern, medical and medication areas are flagged as sensitive; the `🔒 Safeguarding` tab on a child's record is restricted to administrators and the safeguarding lead.
- Paper records — a contemporaneous note, a signed declaration, a court order — are held in a **locked cabinet** accessible only to **[PLACEHOLDER — named roles]**, and are scanned into the system where appropriate.
- Records are **not** held on personal devices, personal email accounts or personal cloud storage, and are not sent by unencrypted email or by messaging app.
- Where a record is exported — the concern PDF, the register download, the onboarding pack — it is handled as a confidential document, transmitted securely, and destroyed when no longer needed. The register's download dialog marks the sensitive columns (collection password, date of birth, school) as **handle with care**, and they are only included where there is a reason.

## 26. Monitoring, review and assurance

### 26.1 Review cycle

- This policy is reviewed **at least annually**, by the DSL, and approved by the senior accountable person.
- It is reviewed **immediately after any significant safeguarding incident**, any allegation against a member of staff, any missing-child incident, any near-miss, and any change to law or guidance.
- It is reviewed on any **material change to our provision** — a new age group, a new site, overnight provision, online delivery, a new school contract, a new video platform.
- **Every September**, the DSL checks whether the guidance this policy relies on has been updated, in particular KCSIE and the out-of-school settings guidance.
- The review date is held on the document record, and the document shows as expiring in advance so that the review is not missed.

### 26.2 What we monitor

The DSL reports at least annually to the senior accountable person on:

- the number and type of concerns recorded, and the outcomes;
- referrals made, and the response;
- any allegation against a member of staff, and its outcome;
- low-level concerns and any pattern in them;
- accidents, near-misses, missing-child and uncollected-child incidents;
- training compliance and any gaps;
- safer-recruitment compliance — anyone working while **On hold**, and why;
- certificate expiry — DBS, safeguarding, first aid;
- outstanding policy read-confirmations;
- themes: are concerns clustering at one site, in one age group, with one staff member, at one time of day?

26.3 **We treat a low number of concerns as a warning sign, not a success.** A setting that records nothing is far more likely to be a setting where nobody reports than a setting where nothing happens.

### 26.4 Learning

After any significant incident, the DSL asks: what happened, what allowed it, what would have caught it earlier, and what changes now — in this policy, in training, in the procedure, or in how we staff the session. The answer is written down and acted on, and progress is checked at the next review.
